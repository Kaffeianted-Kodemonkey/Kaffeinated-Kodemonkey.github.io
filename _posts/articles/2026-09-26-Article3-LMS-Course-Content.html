No Title II Exception for LMS Course Content: Manual Accessibility Audits Required

Password protection alone does not determine whether learning management system content is covered by the Title II web accessibility rule. The rule covers web content public entities provide or make available, subject to specific exceptions.

DOJ’s rule generally covers web content a public entity provides or makes available, including through an arrangement with another provider. The rule has specific, limited exceptions, so LMS materials should be reviewed against the actual regulatory text rather than treated as categorically included or exempt. For Title II, entities with a total population of 50,000 or more have until April 26, 2027; those under 50,000 and special district governments have until April 26, 2028. DOJ explains that school districts are not special district governments and provides guidance for determining their population category. See the DOJ fact sheet and 2026 Interim Final Rule.

Why the “Behind the Login” Assumption Failed

Access controls alone do not determine whether content is covered. The rule applies to web content a public entity provides or makes available, including content provided through contractual or other arrangements, while listing limited exceptions for specified content. Review each LMS, course file, video, interactive module, and third-party tool in light of those provisions and the entity’s Title II responsibilities.

Why Automated Tools Alone Are Insufficient

Complex educational content presents unique barriers that automated scanners routinely miss or misclassify:

  • STEM equations, graphs, and interactive simulations often lack proper text alternatives or keyboard operability.
  • Multimedia lectures require accurate captions, transcripts, and audio descriptions.
  • Timed assessments and interactive quizzes can create timing, focus, and cognitive accessibility failures.
  • Faculty-created materials frequently inherit inaccessible templates or third-party embeds.

For an independent review of an LMS website or related digital product, see our WCAG accessibility audit and POCC documentation. Teams managing frequent course and platform updates can also explore accessibility monitoring with quarterly evaluation.

Automated checks can help identify some issues, but complex course materials may require manual review. The DOJ fact sheet describes WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the technical standard and links to the rule’s detailed requirements and exceptions.

What a Defensible LMS Accessibility Program Looks Like

IT and academic technology leaders should treat LMS accessibility as a multi-year program rather than a one-time project:

  • Inventory at the course and template level: Identify which platforms, course shells, and high-enrollment courses carry the greatest risk.
  • Establish faculty support and content creation standards: Provide accessible templates, clear authoring guidelines, and easy paths for remediation assistance.
  • Require vendor accountability: Demand current, detailed Accessibility Conformance Reports for the LMS itself and for major third-party integrations. Treat “partially supports” or “not evaluated” claims as open risk.
  • Commission independent manual evaluation: DHS Certified Trusted Tester audits remain the gold standard for producing documentation that withstands procurement review, OCR investigation, or private litigation. Automated reports are useful for triage; they are rarely sufficient as primary evidence of conformance.
  • Build continuous processes: New courses, updated materials, and platform upgrades will continually reintroduce barriers unless monitoring and remediation workflows are institutionalized.
